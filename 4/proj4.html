Part 1: Fit a Neural Field to a 2D Image

We first fit a neural field model to a 2D image. I created a MLP network with Sinusoidal Positional Encoding. The input is the 2D pixel coordinates, and we want it to predict the pixel colors (R, G, B).

The image architecture I used is as follows:

  • 3 linear hidden layers with ReLU activation for the hidden layers and a sigmoid output activation
  • Hidden layer width of 256
  • Adam optimizer with learning rate of 1e-2
  • Trained for 3000 iterations

Part 2: Fit a Neural Radiance Field from Multi-view Images

Part 2.1: Create Rays from Cameras

In this part, I created helper functions for the NeRF that convert Camera to World Coordinates, Pixel to Camera Coordinates, and Pixel to Ray. For the camera to world conversion, I first converted camera coordinates into homogenous coordinates, multiplying it by the camera-to-world matrix in row-vector form to get the world coordinates in homogenous coordinate form, then removing the homogenous coordinate by dividing it. Similarly, for the pixel to camera conversion, I constructed homogenous pixel coordinates, inverted the intrinsic matrix K and applied the inverted K matrix in row-vector form.

To find the rays from the pixel, I set the depth to be 1 for all pixels, and used the Camera to World and Pixel to Camera conversion functions to map each pixel to camera space then world space. The ray origin is the camera position given by the translation part of the c2w matrix and I got the ray direction by subtracting this origin from the world coordinates point and normalizing the output.

Part 2.2: Sampling

For ray sampling, I implemented a global sampling approach where I computed the rays for all pixels across all images and then did a random sample from this dataset. I created a class called SampleRaysFromImage and looped over each image-camera pair to create a flattened grid of pixel indices, added 0.5 to the x and y to make them centered by the pixel, and used the camera intrinsics and extrinsics to convert these pixel coordinates into ray origins and directions with the corresponding colors.

To sample the points along each ray, I discretized the segment between a near and far bound (near=2 and far=6 in this case) into a fixed number of depth values and added a small perturbation during training. Then, these depths were converted into 3D points using the ray equation.

Part 2.3: Putting the Dataloading All Together

Using the classes and functions I implemented from the previous parts, I created a dataset from the images and set up a Dataloader that could load the data in batches. For each batch, I call sample_along_rays to get the sampled 3D points along the corresponding rays. Once again, I visualized the rays of the sample points using Viser.

Part 2.4: Neural Radiance Field

For the NeRF model, I created the below network structure:

It takes in the 3D world coordinates of the image and the corresponding ray directions and outputs the density and color (RGB) for each 3D sample. The network is much deeper than the one I created in Part 1 and there is a skip connection that concatenates the encoded input back into the middle layers. For this model, I still use positional encoding on the input, with 8 hidden layers with ReLU activations between, and a final sigmoid activation for the color output.

Part 2.5: Volume Rendering

In volume rendering, I followed the discrete approximation of the core volume rendering equation to get the rendered colors when passing in the densities, colors, and step size.

I trained the NeRF model on the lego dataset by predicting the densities and rgb of the sampled points and using volume rendering to find the pixel colors. Within the training loop, I also kept track of the training and mean validation PSNR values at each iteration.

Here is the architecture and hyperparameters of my model:

  • 8 linear hidden layers with ReLU activation for the hidden layers and a sigmoid output activation
  • Hidden layer width of 256
  • Adam optimizer with learning rate of 5e-4
  • Num_samples = 64, Far = 6, Near = 2
  • Trained for 1500 iterations

With these hyperparameters, I was able to get a training PSNR of 24.15. The validation PSNR was also above 24. Below shows the training progression on one of the validation images as well as the training and validation PSNR graphs:

Part 2.6: Training with your own data

I initially tried training with the dataset captured from Part 0.2. However, the results were unideal as I believe I captured from too many angles and not enough from a consistent circular trajectory around the object. Thus, I went back and recaptured the images.

After recapturing the dataset and A LOT of trial and error, I decided to train the model on 4000 iterations with Near=0.1, Far=0.6, Num_samples=64. The other hyperparameters are the same as the previous part (Adam Optimizer, LR=5e-4). This reached a training PSNR of around 19. However, the gifs generated on the x, y, and z rotation scale were still very blurry.

Thus, I trained the model on another 2000 iterations for a total of 6000 iterations, which brought the PSNR up to 20 - below is the result. The NeRF reconstruction on a singular image, although not ideal, didn’t look too horrible. However, when rotated around the x, y, and z axis for a circular trajectory the outputs were very blurry.

I increased the training for another 2000 iterations (now a total of 8000 iterations). The PSNR didn’t seem to increase anymore, staggering at a little above 20. However, the NeRF output did look better.

As results weren't ideal, I went back and cropped all the training images so they were closer on the object and the tag. The results actually shockingly became way better - even after just 2000 iterations of training. Although the PSNR value staggered at 17 after ~1000 epochs, the novel view point actually looked better.
